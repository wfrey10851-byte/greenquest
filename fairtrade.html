Core Foundations

Q1: What is fair trade?

Fair trade is a trade arrangement designed to help producers in developing countries achieve sustainable and equitable conditions.

Q2: What is fair trade advocating?

The fair trade movement advocates paying higher prices to exporters and improving social and environmental standards.

5.2 History of Fair Trade

1940s – 1950s

The Early Religious & NGO Roots

The first attempts to commercialize fair trade goods in markets in the global north were initiated in the 1940s and 1950s by religious groups and various politically oriented non-governmental organizations (NGOs).

Ten Thousand Villages (an NGO within the Mennonite Central Committee) and SERRV International were the first, in 1946 and 1949 respectively, to develop fair trade supply chains in developing countries. The products, almost exclusively handicrafts ranging from jute goods to cross-stitch work, were mostly sold in churches or fairs.

The Donation Function Era

During this initial historical stage, the early alternative goods themselves had often no other function than to indicate that a donation had been made.

1980s Market Crisis

The Shift: Handicrafts vs. Agricultural Goods

In the early 1980s, alternative trading organizations faced structural challenges: the novelty of fair trade products began to wear off, demand reached a plateau and some handicrafts began to look "tired and old fashioned" in the marketplace.

"The decline of segments of the handicrafts market forced fair trade supporters to rethink their business model and their goals. Several supporters were deeply worried by the effect on small farmers of structural reforms in the agricultural sector as well as the fall in commodity prices."

Many came to believe it was the movement's responsibility to address the issue and deploy usable remedies. In subsequent years, fair trade agricultural commodities played an important role in the growth of many ATOs: successful on the market, they offered a source of income for producers and provided alternative trading organizations a complement to the handicrafts market.

Brand Procurement Vector Alignment

If you were the coffee brand, what would you do to navigate structural procurement vulnerabilities?

Ending 2: Profit-First Ending

You successfully cut procurement transaction sourcing costs. Corporate profits increase immediately. But one year later…

  • Farmers exit coffee production entirely due to insolvency
  • Raw material bean supply becomes highly volatile and unstable
  • Consumer store shelf coffee prices start to fluctuate wildly
Key Takeaway: When everyone races to the lowest price, the whole system becomes fragile.

Ending 3: Fair Trade Ending

You introduce a guaranteed non-negotiable minimum price for upstream smallholder farmers.

Short-term margins are slightly adjusted, but your structural logistics secure major long-term ecosystem benefits:

  • Upstream smallholder farmer income indicators stabilize securely
  • Raw material logistics pipelines become highly reliable and resilient
  • Your procurement brand secures consumer trust and long-term asset value
Key Takeaway: Fair Trade helps match rewards with the risks producers take.

5.4 Beneficiaries & Limitations

Coffee is the most well-established fair trade commodity. Most Fair Trade coffee is Coffea arabica, which is grown at high altitudes. Fair Trade markets emphasize the quality of coffee because they usually appeal to customers who are motivated by taste rather than price.

The fair trade movement fixated on coffee first because it is a highly traded commodity for most producing countries, and almost half the world's coffee is produced by smallholder farmers. At first fair trade coffee was sold at small scale; now multinationals like Starbucks and Nestlé use fair trade coffee. As of 2006, Starbucks was the world's largest purchaser of fair trade coffee.

Institutional Logistics Pipelines

Internationally recognized Fair Trade coffee standards outlined by FLO (Fairtrade International) are structured as follows: small producers are grouped in democratic cooperatives or groups; buyers and sellers establish long-term, stable relationships; buyers pay the producers at least the minimum Fair Trade price or, when the market price is higher, the market price.

The marketing system for fair trade and non-fair trade coffee is identical in the consuming and developing countries, using mostly the same importing, packing, distributing, and retailing firms used worldwide. Some independent brands operate a "virtual company", paying importers, packers and distributors, and advertising agencies to handle their brand, for cost reasons.

Market Disproportion & Boundaries

In the producing country, fair trade is marketed only by certified fair trade cooperatives, while other coffee is marketed by fair trade cooperatives (as uncertified coffee), by other cooperatives and by ordinary traders. This creates structural parallel pipelines that limit the total execution volume.
